# 📐 Shannon Entropy

### 📖 Overview

After understanding **Information Theory**, **Random Variables**, and **Probability**, we are now ready to answer one of the most fundamental questions in Information Theory:

> **How can uncertainty be measured mathematically?**

Claude Shannon answered this question by introducing **Shannon Entropy**, a mathematical measure of the **average uncertainty** or **average information** produced by a random source.

Entropy is one of the most important concepts in cryptography because the security of passwords, cryptographic keys, and secure communication depends on unpredictability. Higher entropy indicates greater uncertainty, making it more difficult for an attacker to predict or infer secret information.

***

### 🎯 Why Study Shannon Entropy?

Shannon Entropy enables us to:

* Measure uncertainty mathematically.
* Quantify the amount of information generated by a random source.
* Compare different probability distributions.
* Evaluate randomness in cryptographic systems.
* Analyze password strength and key security.
* Understand the mathematical foundation of Information-Theoretic Security.

***

### 🌍 Real-World Motivation

Imagine two situations.

#### Situation 1

You are told:

> "The sun will rise tomorrow."

This message provides almost no new information because the event is almost certain.

#### Situation 2

You are told:

> "You have won an international lottery."

This message provides a large amount of information because the event was highly unlikely.

These examples illustrate an important observation:

* Predictable events provide little information.
* Rare events provide more information.

This naturally leads to the question:

> **Can we assign a numerical value to the information contained in an event?**

Claude Shannon answered **Yes**.

***

### 📈 Information Content

The amount of information provided by an event is called its **Information Content**.

It depends entirely on the probability of that event.

| Probability | Information Content |
| ----------- | ------------------- |
| Very High   | Very Small          |
| Medium      | Moderate            |
| Very Low    | Very Large          |

Therefore:

* Less probable events contain **more information**.
* More probable events contain **less information**.

***

### 🔢 Self Information

The information associated with a **single event** is called **Self Information**.

It is mathematically defined as:

$$
I(x)=-\log_2(P(x))
$$

where:

* $$I(x)$$ = Self Information
* $$P(x)$$ = Probability of event $x$
* $$\log_2$$ = Logarithm to base 2

***

#### Interpretation

**Event A**

Suppose

$$
P(A)=1
$$

Then,

$$
I(A)=0
$$

A certain event carries **no new information**.

***

**Event B**

Suppose

$$
P(B)=0.5
$$

The event is less predictable.

Therefore, it contains **more information** than Event A.

***

**Event C**

Suppose

$$
P(C)=0.001
$$

This event is extremely unlikely.

Therefore, it provides a **very large amount of information**.

***

### ➕ Why Average Information?

Real-world communication systems generate **many events**, not just one.

Consider a random source:

| Outcome | Probability |
| ------- | ----------: |
| A       |        0.50 |
| B       |        0.30 |
| C       |        0.20 |

Each outcome carries a different amount of information.

Therefore, the overall uncertainty should be measured using the **average information** produced by all possible outcomes.

This idea leads directly to **Shannon Entropy**.

***

### 📖 Definition of Shannon Entropy

**Shannon Entropy** is the **average amount of information** produced by a random source.

It can also be interpreted as the **average uncertainty** before observing the outcome of a random event.

***

### 📐 Mathematical Derivation

The derivation of Shannon Entropy begins with the concept of **Self Information**.

For a single event,

$$
I(x)= -\log_2(P(x))
$$

Each event occurs with probability $P(x)$.

To determine the average information produced by an entire random source, we use the concept of **Expected Value**.

Therefore,

$$
H(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(x_i)\times I(x_i)
$$

Substituting the expression for Self Information,

$$
I(x_i)= -\log_2(P(x_i))
$$

gives

$$
H(X)=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
P(x_i)\left(-\log_2(P(x_i))\right)
$$

Taking the negative sign outside the summation,

$$
H(X)=-
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
P(x_i)\log_2(P(x_i))
$$

This is known as the **Shannon Entropy Formula**.

***

### 📏 Shannon Entropy Formula

$$
\boxed{
H(X)=-
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
P(x_i)\log_2(P(x_i))
}
$$

where:

* $$H(X)$$ = Shannon Entropy
* $$P(x_i)$$ = Probability of the $i^{th}$ outcome
* $$\log_2$$ = Logarithm to base 2
* $$n$$= Number of possible outcomes

Entropy is measured in **bits**.

***

### ➖ Why the Negative Sign?

Probabilities satisfy

$$
0<P(x)\le1
$$

The logarithm of numbers between **0** and **1** is always negative.

For example,

$$
\log_2(0.5)=-1
$$

Without the negative sign, entropy would become negative.

Since uncertainty cannot be negative, Shannon introduced the negative sign to ensure entropy is always non-negative.

***

### 🔢 Why Logarithm?

Shannon used logarithms because they satisfy several desirable mathematical properties.

For example, if two independent events occur, their probabilities multiply.

Logarithms convert multiplication into addition, making information easier to analyze mathematically.

***

### 2️⃣ Why Base-2?

Information in digital computers is represented using **binary digits (bits)**.

Using base-2 logarithms means entropy is measured directly in **bits**.

Other logarithmic bases are also possible.

| Logarithm     | Unit     |
| ------------- | -------- |
| $$\log_2$$    | Bits     |
| $$\ln$$       | Nats     |
| $$\log_{10}$$ | Hartleys |

***

### 📦 Units of Entropy

Depending on the logarithm used, entropy may be measured in:

| Base     | Unit     |
| -------- | -------- |
| Base 2   | Bits     |
| Base _e_ | Nats     |
| Base 10  | Hartleys |

In cryptography, **bits** are almost always used.

### 🌍 Cybersecurity Perspective

Shannon Entropy is widely used in cybersecurity for:

* Password strength analysis
* Cryptographic key evaluation
* Random number generation
* Malware detection
* Information leakage analysis
* Secure communication protocols

Higher entropy generally corresponds to stronger security because attackers face greater uncertainty.

***

### ⚠️ Common Mistakes

* Confusing **Self Information** with **Shannon Entropy**.
* Forgetting the negative sign.
* Using probabilities that do not sum to **1**.
* Using the wrong logarithm base.
* Assuming entropy directly measures security rather than uncertainty.

***

### 📌 Key Takeaways

* Shannon Entropy measures **average uncertainty**.
* Information depends on probability.
* Rare events contain more information.
* Entropy is the expected value of Self Information.
* Entropy is measured in **bits** when using base-2 logarithms.

### 📝 Page Summary

Shannon Entropy is a mathematical measure of the average uncertainty produced by a random source. Starting from the concept of Self Information, Shannon derived entropy as the expected information generated by all possible outcomes of a random variable. The resulting formula provides a quantitative measure of uncertainty and forms the foundation of modern Information Theory, cryptography, and secure communication systems.
